Reinsurance, explained for the person building the pipeline

01Orientation

Reinsurance is insurance for insurance companies. Everything else is detail on top of that one sentence.

An insurer (the cedent) takes on risk from policyholders. Some of that risk is bigger than it wants to keep — a single factory, a hurricane, an entire book of motor policies. So it buys protection from a reinsurer, which agrees to take a defined share of the losses in exchange for a defined share of the premium. The reinsurer may in turn buy retrocession, which is reinsurance of reinsurance.

The value chain in one line each

LinkWhat they doWhat they send you
PolicyholderBuys a policy, pays premiumNothing directly — arrives via the cedent
Cedent (insurer)Underwrites and administers the book, cedes part of itBordereaux, premium and loss advices
BrokerPlaces the treaty, sometimes administers the accountPlacing slips, account statements
ReinsurerAccepts the cession, holds the riskConfirmations, claims settlements
RetrocessionaireReinsures the reinsurerRetro bordereaux — same shape, one level up

Why this matters to a data engineer

Almost every reinsurance process is a data exchange between two companies, and neither company controls the other's format, timing or quality. That is the single fact that shapes your job. A policy admin system is mostly internal and predictable. A reinsurance feed is external, late, inconsistently keyed, and financially material.

  • You are usually the reconciliation layer. The cedent says one number, the reinsurer's ledger says another, and someone has to explain the difference by treaty, period and currency.
  • Late data is normal, not an exception. Design for restatement from day one.
  • Precision is period-dependent. The same table is "good enough" in a soft close and "must tie to the penny" in a hard close. Your pipeline needs to know which one it is in.
  • Keys are ugly. Treaty numbers, account numbers and claim references are free text in spreadsheets and vary by cedent.
The mental model to keep: a reinsurance data platform is a set of per-treaty accounts. Every premium, commission, loss and recovery ultimately lands on one account for one period, and that account has to balance to the counterparty's version of the same account.

03Treaty structures

Two families: proportional, where premium and losses are shared by percentage, and non-proportional, where the reinsurer pays only above a threshold.

Proportional

The reinsurer takes a fixed share of everything on the covered portfolio — the same share of premium, the same share of losses.

  • Quota share (QS) — a flat percentage, e.g. 40% of every policy. Simplest to model: one cession percentage applied to premium and loss lines.
  • Surplus — the cedent keeps a retention line per risk and cedes the excess up to N lines. Small risks may be unceded entirely; a large risk can be heavily ceded. Cession is therefore row-level, not one percentage for the file.
  • Facultative-obligatory (fac oblig) — the cedent must offer, the reinsurer may accept on a per-risk basis. Produces a per-policy acceptance flag your pipeline must respect.

Non-proportional

The reinsurer pays nothing until losses cross an attachment point, then pays up to a limit. This is excess of loss (XL).

  • Per risk XL — applies to each risk separately, protects frequency of medium losses.
  • Per occurrence / cat XL — one event, many policies. A hurricane triggers one recovery across thousands of rows, so the calculation is an aggregation, not a lookup.
  • Aggregate stop loss — protects the whole year's loss ratio above a threshold.

Recovery = MIN(MAX(loss − attachment, 0), limit). Below the attachment point the cedent keeps the whole loss — a very common reconciliation surprise when a small claim is booked as a recovery.

Data-engineering consequence. A quota share cedes with one join and one multiplication. A surplus treaty needs a per-row retention and line capacity. A cat XL needs an event identifier on every claim before you can compute anything. The treaty type dictates the grain of your fact table — decide it before you write the model.
Facultative vs treaty, in one paragraph

Treaty reinsurance is automatic: every risk inside the agreed definition is ceded without asking. Facultative is manual: the cedent offers an individual risk and the reinsurer accepts or declines it. In data terms, treaty cessions are derived (compute from the policy and the treaty terms) whereas facultative cessions are received facts you must load and cannot recompute. Mixing the two in one table is a classic modelling mistake.

04Money flows

Four flows cover most of the domain: premium in, commission back, losses out, recoveries in.

FlowDirectionWhat it isWhere it breaks
PremiumCedent → reinsurerShare of written premium, often paid as a deposit premium on account with a true-up laterDeposit vs actual; instalments; cancellations
Ceding commissionReinsurer → cedentContribution to the cedent's acquisition costs, at an agreed rateSliding scale, profit commission, provisional vs final rate
Loss / claimReinsurer → cedentReinsurer's share of a paid claimAdvice arrives after the cedent has already paid
Recovery / salvageCedent → reinsurerSubrogation or salvage returned, reduces the lossTiming gap; netting conventions differ by cedent

Terms that change the arithmetic

Sliding-scale commission

The commission rate moves with the loss ratio. A good year earns a higher rate, a bad year a lower one — so the rate is not knowable until the period's losses are known. This is one of the main reasons reinsurance periods stay open.

Profit commission

After a defined margin and expense allowance, a percentage of the remaining profit goes back to the cedent. It is a calculation over the whole account for the year, never a row-level field.

Experience account / deposit account

A running ledger between the parties: premium in, losses and commission out, balance carried forward, sometimes with interest. If you model it as a stateless monthly figure you will be wrong; it is a balance that needs correct opening and closing values.

Cash loss

A large claim the cedent notifies immediately, before the formal bordereau, so cash moves fast. Cash losses typically arrive as their own feed with their own reference numbers and must later be matched to the bordereau line that reports the same claim. Unmatched cash losses are a permanent source of double counting.

Design rule: keep cash-loss advices and bordereau claims in separate tables with an explicit link and a match status. Never merge them on arrival — merge them when they reconcile, and expose the unmatched ones as a control.

05Soft close vs hard close

The distinction that decides how much precision your pipeline owes, and whether the period can still change under you.

Both words describe how final a period's numbers are. They are not two processes; they are two stopping points on the same process.

Soft close

A fast, provisional pass. Book the material and known items, estimate the rest, leave the period open for true-ups. Purpose: get numbers in front of management and treaty managers early.

  • Missing bordereaux estimated from prior periods
  • Commission at expected rates
  • IBNR rolled forward, not refreshed
  • Late items posted next period

Hard close

The full pass. Every account reconciled, estimates replaced by actuals, signed off, and the period locked. Later corrections become adjustments in the next period, not re-posts.

  • All bordereaux received and agreed
  • Adjustable features finalised where possible
  • Recoverables confirmed with counterparty
  • Audit trail, lock, no reopening without exception
Day 1–3 · Data cut and soft close Load what has arrived, estimate the gaps, produce provisional treaty accounts.
Day 3–5 · Review and challenge Treaty managers explain movements; large breaks get investigated, not smoothed.
Day 5–10 · Late data and true-up Bordereaux arrive, estimates are reversed and replaced, experience accounts recalculated.
Quarter / year end · Hard close Reconciliations signed off, reserves refreshed, period locked, statutory and management views aligned.

What a data engineer must build differently for each

ConcernSoft closeHard close
Late dataAbsorbed in the next run, no dramaFormal restatement with a reason code
EstimatesPermitted, flagged as provisionalMust be replaced by actuals or justified
ReproducibilityNice to haveMandatory — the number you signed must be reproducible
Edits to closed periodsAllowedBlocked by policy; only adjustments forward
AuditLight loggingFull lineage, who ran what, with what inputs
The one rule worth memorising. A soft close may be approximate; a hard close must be reproducible. If you cannot re-run the job with the same inputs and get the same signed number, the hard close is not defensible — regardless of whether the number was right.
Confusing look-alikes: soft/hard "market" and system close states

Soft market / hard market is the underwriting cycle: soft = excess capacity, falling rates, looser terms; hard = capacity withdrawn, rates rising. Nothing to do with closing a period.

Soft close / hard close in a policy admin system is the same idea expressed as state: soft close warns but permits further postings; hard close blocks them. Same semantics, applied to a system flag rather than a finance calendar.

06The data model

Seven entities carry most reinsurance platforms. Get the grain and the keys right and the rest is plumbing.

EntityGrainKeyNotes
cedentone companycedent_idAlso holds reporting calendar and currency conventions
treatyone contract, versionedtreaty_id + valid_fromTerms change by year; keep versions, never overwrite
treaty_sectionone layer or sharetreaty_id, section_noOne treaty can hold several layers with different maths
bordereau_headerone submissioncedent_id, report_period, sequenceRestatements create a new sequence for the same period
bordereau_lineone policy or claim rowheader key + line_noFree-text references; expect duplicates across resubmissions
claim_noticeone claim eventclaim_ref (cedent-scoped)Cash losses arrive before the bordereau reporting the same claim
cession_factone accounting movementcomposite + close_periodPremium, commission, loss, recovery as rows — one shape, many types

Modelling choices that save you later

  • Treaty is slowly changing, not static. Model it SCD2. A retroactive term change must be visible as a version with its own effect, or every historic number silently moves.
  • One movement table beats many. Premium, commission, loss and recovery differ by sign and type, not by structure. A single cession_fact with a movement_type keeps reconciliation and reporting simple.
  • Bitemporal, because the domain is. You need "what we knew on the close date" (valid time in the business sense) and "what the file said when we loaded it" (system time). A hard close signs a snapshot of the first, and audit asks about the second.
  • Currency is a fact, not a label. Store original currency, amount, rate, rate date and source. Retrospective rate changes are a real reconciliation category.
  • Never overwrite a closed period row. Append a correction with a link to the row it corrects and a reason code.
CREATE TABLE cession_fact (
  cession_id      BIGINT,
  treaty_id       BIGINT      NOT NULL,
  section_no      INT         NOT NULL,
  cedent_id       BIGINT      NOT NULL,
  policy_ref      VARCHAR(64),
  claim_ref       VARCHAR(64),
  movement_type   VARCHAR(16) NOT NULL,  -- PREMIUM | COMMISSION | LOSS | RECOVERY
  underwriting_yr INT         NOT NULL,
  accounting_period DATE      NOT NULL,
  currency        CHAR(3)     NOT NULL,
  amount_orig     DECIMAL(18,2) NOT NULL,
  fx_rate         DECIMAL(18,8) NOT NULL,
  amount_usd      DECIMAL(18,2) NOT NULL,
  close_run_id    BIGINT      NOT NULL,  -- which close produced this row

-- A closed period must be immutable: enforce it, do not rely on discipline.
CREATE TABLE close_period (
  accounting_period DATE PRIMARY KEY,
  status            VARCHAR(12) NOT NULL,   -- OPEN | SOFT_CLOSED | HARD_CLOSED
  closed_at         TIMESTAMP,
  closed_by         VARCHAR(64),
  run_id            BIGINT
);

# Treaty terms arrive with effective dates; keep every version.
w = Window.partitionBy("treaty_id").orderBy("valid_from")

new_versions = (
    incoming.alias("i")
    .join(current.alias("c"), "treaty_id", "left")
    .filter(
        (F.col("c.treaty_id").isNull()) |
        (F.col("i.valid_from") > F.col("c.valid_from"))

    .select("i.*")
)

# Close the previous version the day before the new one starts.
closed = (
    current.alias("c")
    .join(new_versions.alias("n"), "treaty_id", "inner")
    .select(
        *[F.col(f"c.{col}") for col in current.columns],
        F.date_sub(F.col("n.valid_from"), 1).alias("valid_to"),
        F.lit(True).alias("is_current"),
    )

07Pipeline patterns

Six patterns that come up in almost every reinsurance build, with the shape of the code.

1 · Bordereaux ingestion is not a CSV load

Every cedent sends a different layout, often as an Excel file with merged headers and blank rows, named something like BORD_MAR26_v2_FINAL.xlsx. Land the file immutably, hash it, then parse a copy. The hash is your only defence against the same file being loaded twice under a different name.

2 · Idempotency by natural key, not by run time

Reprocessing a period must not double the premium. Deduplicate on (cedent, report_period, sequence, line_no) and write with a deterministic merge, so the second run is a no-op.

-- Idempotent load: rerun-safe by natural key
MERGE INTO bordereau_line AS t
USING staged_lines AS s
  ON  t.cedent_id      = s.cedent_id
  AND t.report_period  = s.report_period
  AND t.sequence       = s.sequence
  AND t.line_no        = s.line_no
WHEN MATCHED AND s.row_hash <> t.row_hash THEN UPDATE SET *
WHEN NOT MATCHED THEN INSERT *;

3 · Late-arriving data is the default case

A bordereau for March can arrive in June. Do not "fix" the March partition in place. Load it with its report_period (business time) and its received_at (arrival time), and let the close process decide whether to restate.

4 · Two clocks, always

Every table gets report_period (what the data is about) and loaded_at (when we learned it). Without both you cannot answer "what did the hard close see on 5 April".

5 · Cession calculation is a service, not a spreadsheet

It will be reimplemented by three people in Excel unless you own it. Expose it as one function with treaty terms as input and ceded amount as output, and test it against known cases — share, surplus retention, XL below attachment, XL capped by limit.

6 · Close runs are versioned artefacts

Every close produces a run_id. The number signed at hard close must be traceable to that run, its inputs and its code commit. This is not bureaucracy; it is the only way to survive the question "why is this different from last month's version of the same period".

Data-quality checks that pay for themselves: premium lines with no matching treaty; cession percentage outside 0–100; loss recovery where gross loss is below the attachment point; duplicate claim references across cash-loss and bordereau feeds; bordereau totals that do not equal the sum of their lines; period-end accruals with no reversal in the next period.

08Reconciliation and controls

In reinsurance, "the pipeline ran" is not the same as "the numbers agree". Reconciliation is the product.

You are reconciling three things at once, and confusing them is common:

  • Internal consistency — does the bordereau total equal the sum of its lines?
  • Counterparty agreement — does your calculated cession equal what the cedent says it is?
  • Period-to-period movement — is the change explainable by new business, cancellations, late data and true-ups?

A break taxonomy worth having from day one

Break typeTypical causeWho fixes it
TimingBordereau arrived after the cut-offClose process — restate
Reference / keyClaim or policy reference formatted differentlyData engineer — mapping rules
Rate / termTreaty version applied is not the one in forceTreaty admin — confirm version
CalculationRetention, attachment or limit applied wronglyData engineer — fix logic, retest
FXDifferent rate or rate dateAgree rate source with finance
Genuine discrepancyCounterparty error or disputed itemTreaty manager — raise with cedent
Emit the reconciliation, don't just run it. Produce a break table with period, treaty, counterparty, expected, actual, difference, type and owner. A break list that nobody can filter by owner is a break list nobody closes.
